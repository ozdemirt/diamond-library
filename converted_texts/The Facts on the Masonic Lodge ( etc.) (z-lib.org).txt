Taking a Serious and Honest Look
Is the Masonic Lodge compatible with Christianity? The Lodge
challenged The John Ankerberg Show about its teachings on Masonry.
They maintained that Masonry was not a religion and did not conflict
with Christianity. But is this true?
In this book, we have cited Masonic Ritual-the authoritative source
for all Masons-and shown how it conflicts with Christian teaching. We
also contacted all 50 of the Grand Lodges in the United States and
asked them which Masonic authors and books they would recommend
as the most authoritative on the teachings of Masonry. We will share
in this book what those that responded said. We will then compare the
commentary of their recommended authors with some of the
teachings of biblical Christianity to answer the question, "Is
Christianity compatible with the teachings of the Masonic Lodge?"
The information we present is fully documented from authoritative
Masonic sources and is confirmed as accurate by Masons and former
Masons.
In this book, we have stressed the Ritual of the Blue Lodge-the first
three degrees of Masonry (Entered Apprentice, Fellow Craft, and
Master Mason)-since these are the degrees through which every Mason
must pass and because most Masons complete only these three
degrees.
We have examined the standard interpretations of the Blue Lodge
Ritual given by Masons. Though most Masons believe that Blue Lodge
Masonry makes one as full or complete a Mason as one can (or needs)
to be, an important fact must be noted. While the Blue Lodge is
Masonry, and while it is the Masonry of most Masons, it is not all that
Masonry constitutes. Some Masons view Blue Lodge Masonry as it is
usually interpreted as an initial or beginning form of Masonry. They
maintain that the real substance of Masonry-its lifeblood-lies in the
higher degrees and in the initiate's search for their true meaning.
Some Masons even consider Blue Lodge Masonry only the cover of the
book, not the book itself. They would say that to truly understand
Masonry a person must open the book and read what lies within the
cover.
As Sovereign Grand Commander Henry C. Clausen commented, "It
must be apparent that the Blue Lodge... degrees cannot explain the
whole of Masonry. They are the foundation... An initiate may imagine

Masonry-also known as Freemasonry, or "the Lodge"-is a powerful,
centuries-old fraternal order that began in the early eighteenth
century. According to most Masonic authorities, modern Masonry-also
called "speculative" Masonry-can be traced to the founding of the first
Grand Lodge in London in AD 1717 (70,1:131,152; 1:3; 15:12).
The Lodge is also a secret society. To maintain its secrets, Masonry
uses symbolism, secret oaths, and secret rituals to instruct new
members, called initiates. Each new member swears during these
secret ceremonies to remain loyal to the Lodge and its teachings. The
teachings instruct each new candidate how he is to serve and the
rewards he can expect.
If we examine the definition of Masonry as given by Masons
themselves, we find the following. In The Revised Encyclopedia of
Freemasonry, Albert G. Mackey, one of the most respected Masonic
scholars, declares, "All [Masons] unite in declaring it to be a system of
morality, by the practice of which its members may advance their
spiritual interest, and mount by the theological ladder from the Lodge
on earth to the Lodge in heaven" (96,1:269).
Other definitions by respected Masonic authorities say that Masonry
is
• "a science which is engaged in the search after Divine Truth, and
which employs symbolism as its method of instruction"
(96,1:269).
• "that religious and mystical society whose aim is moral
perfection on the basis of general equality and fraternity" (96,
1:269).
• "in its broadest and most comprehensive sense ...a system of

morality and social ethics, a primitive religion, and a philosophy
of life... incorporating a broad humanitarianism... It is a religion
without a creed, being of no sect but finding truth in all... It seeks
truth but does not define truth" (36:234).
A man who becomes a Mason is defined as "one who has been
initiated into the mysteries of the fraternity of Freemasonry"
(96,1:378).
What we present in this book is an analysis of Masonry itself, as
presented by Masonic authorities recommended to us by a large group
of the Grand Lodges in the United States (see question 2).

What is the final authority for the
teachings
presented in each Masonic Lodge?
If anyone is going to investigate the teachings of the Masonic Lodge,
who or what is the authority that they should listen to?
When we asked this question on our television program of Bill
Mankin, a thirty-second-degree Mason, he said, "The authoritative
source for Masonry is the Ritual. The Ritual-what happens in the
Lodge, what goes on" (1:3,5).
The Grand Lodge of each state sets the Ritual and the interpretation
of that Ritual that is to be followed by the members of that state.
When a person examines Masonry today and compares the different
manuals containing the Ritual for each state, it is apparent that, today,
the Ritual and the interpretations given are almost identical.
Therefore, the Ritual in the Masonic manuals can be considered the
authoritative teachings of the Lodge. As former Worshipful Master
Jack Harris comments, "In [all] other states... the principle and the
doctrines of the Ritual] are exactly the same. The wording only varies
slightly" (13:29).

4
What are the Blue Lodge, the
Scottish Rite, and the York Rite?
All men who become Masons go through the first three degrees of
the Blue Lodge. The Blue Lodge is the parent, or mother, Lodge of
Freemasonry. In the Blue Lodge are conferred the first three degrees:
1) the Entered Apprentice, where a man is initiated into the beginning
mysteries of the fraternity of Freemasonry; 2) the degree of Fellow
Craft; and 3) the Master Mason degree.
After passing these three degrees in the Blue Lodge, the candidate
may choose not to proceed further at all, or he may choose to advance
higher along one or both of two branches in Masonry.
One branch is known as the Scottish Rite, which progresses by
numerical degrees beginning with the fourth and ending with the
thirty-second, the thirtythird degree usually indicating honorary
status. The other major branch is the York Rite, which does not
number its degrees. It goes through what are called the "Chapter,"
"Council," and "Commandery" degrees, ending with the degree of
Knights Templar.
If a Mason is suspended or expelled from his Blue Lodge, it
automatically severs his connection from all other Masonic bodies.
Anyone who passes the first three degrees and becomes a Master
Mason may visit Blue Lodges other than his own.

Is Freemasonry another religion?
There were approximately 1.56 million Masons in the United States
as of 2006 (115). Many Masons are Christians, and many are from
other religious faiths. The question is, "Are those members of the
Masonic Lodge knowingly or unknowingly participating in another
religion-the religion of Freemasonry?"
Most Masons are adamant in stating that Freemasonry is not a
religion. Masonic author Alphonse Cerza, former Grand Historian of
the Grand Lodge of Illinois, and many other Masons who have written
to us have argued that Freemasonry is not a religion because
1. it does not meet the definition of a religion;
2. it offers no system or teaching of salvation;
3. it has no creed, no confession of faith, no theology, and no
ritual of worship; and
4. it has no symbols that are religious, like the symbols that are
found in a church (1:2, cf. 73:41).
To quote thirty-second-degree Mason Bill Mankin: "All we are
saying is that if you as an individual adopt the principles represented
[in Freemasonry] ... that you will be a better person. Not that you are
going to go to heaven" (1:2). Alphonse Cerza, in his book Let There Be
Light-A Study in AntiMasonry, quoted Dr. M.W. Thomas S. Roy, Grand
Master of the Grand Lodge in Massachusetts, in his address to that
Lodge, who declared, "By any definition of religion accepted by our
critics, we cannot qualify as a religion" (73:41).
To see if Cerza, Roy, and other Masons are correct, let us begin with
the definition of religion from Webster's New World Dictionary:
1) [a] belief in a divine or superhuman power.. .to be obeyed and
worshipped as the Creator and ruler of the universe; 2) expression
of...[this] belief in conduct and ritual.
Now, could any Mason reasonably deny that Freemasonry fits this
definition of religion as given by Webster? Is it not true that Masonry
demands belief in a Supreme Being? Would any Mason deny that

Masonry's authoritative Ritual describes exactly how they are to
express this belief in conduct and ceremony? In brief, can any
Freemason say Masonry is not a religion? The answer is obviously
"No."
But Masons do not need to take our word for it. They only need to
listen to their respected Masonic authorities. As we saw earlier, the
number-one authority recommended by the Grand Lodges was Henry
Wilson Coil's Masonic Encyclopedia. Coil quotes the definition of
religion given by Funk and Wagnalls New Standard Dictionary (1941)
and then asserts that Freemasonry fits not only this definition, but also
the dictionary definition of what constitutes a "church." Coil expressly
uses the phrase "the religion of Freemasonry" (95:512), and he
declares,
Freemasonry certainly requires a belief in the existence of,
and man's dependence upon, a Supreme Being to whom he is
responsible. What can a church add to that, except to bring
into one fellowship those who have like feel- ings?...That is
exactly what the Lodge does (95:512).
In other words, Coil makes it clear that not only is Freemasonry a
religion, but also that it functions as a religion as much as a church
does.
In Mackey's Revised Encyclopedia of Freemasonry, Albert Mackey,
the third most recommended author, quotes Webster's definition of
religion and then comments, "Freemasonry may rightfully claim to be
called a religious institution" (96,11:847).
So is Freemasonry a religion? According to Webster, according to
Funk and Wagnalls, and according to leading Masonic authorities Coil
and Mackey, Freemasonry is a religion.

Does the Masonic Lodge teach
its own plan of salvation?
Another reason Masons give as to why Freemasonry cannot be
considered a religion is because "it offers no system of salvation" (1:2).
In other words, they say Freemasonry has no teachings about how a
man can go to heaven. But is this true?
Every candidate who enters the Blue Lodge is told again and again
during the first three degrees that God will reward those who do good
deeds. This can be documented by examining any Masonic manual
that contains the Ritual of the first three degrees. In the manual under
the explanation of the symbol of the "All-Seeing Eye"-one of the
symbols for God-you will find these words: The "All-Seeing Eye
[God]... beholds [or "pervades" (69:83)] the inmost recesses of the
human heart, and will reward us according to our works" (58:129,
emphasis added).
What is the reward for good works? Masonry teaches that God will
reward man with eternal life in the "Celestial Lodge Above." This can
be documented in the Masonic Ritual and Monitor under the
explanation of the lambskin, or white linen apron:
He who wears the lambskin as a badge of a Mason is thereby
continually reminded of purity of life and conduct which is
essentially necessary to his gaining admission into that
celestial Lodge above, where the Supreme Architect of the
universe presides (58:50, emphasis added; see 69:88).
Does this sound to you as though Freemasonry is teaching a way of
salvation? If you were to hear this taught in the Lodge, wouldn't you
think that Freemasonry is saying that you can go to the "Celestial
Lodge Above" if you live a pure and honest life? Isn't that religion?
If you're a Christian...
When the Lodge teaches a man that by his good life and by his good
deeds God will admit him into heaven, don't you find that contrary to
biblical teaching? Doesn't the Bible clearly teach that salvation is not
by a man's work-it comes only by God's gracious provision through

Jesus Christ? Ephesians 2:8-9 clearly says, "It is by grace you have
been saved, through faith-and this not from yourselves, it is the gift of
God-not by works, so that no one can boast" (Niv).
If you are still not persuaded that Masonry is presenting a way to
heaven, consider Henry Wilson Coil's comments about one of
Freemasonry's religious services. In his encyclopedia he argues,
Freemasonry has a religious service to commit the body of a

the liberated spirit back to the Great Source of Light. Many
Freemasons make this flight with no other guarantee of a safe

From this evidence, everyone must conclude that Freemasonry is a
religion because it offers religious instruction and promises of how a

Does the creed of the Masonic

Some Masons say, along with Masonic authority Alphonse Cerza,
"Freemasonry cannot be a religion because it has no creed; it has no
confession of faith; it has no theology, no ritual of worship" (1:2;
73:41). Let us now examine the claim that Freemasonry cannot be a
religion because it has no creed.
Webster defines creed as "a statement of belief, principles, or
opinions on any subject." According to Webster, how can any Mason
really say he has no creed? No man can become a Mason without
confessing his faith in a Supreme Being. Every Mason must believe in
the immortality of the soul, give honorable service to God by
practicing the secret arts of Masonry, say prayers to deity, and swear
oaths of secrecy in God's name. These practices prove Masons have a
definite creed.
In Coil's Masonic Encyclopedia we find the following:
Does Freemasonry have a creed ...or tenet... or dogma... to
which all members must adhere? Does Freemasonry
continually teach and insist upon a creed, tenet and dogma?
Does it have meetings characterized by the practice of rites
and ceremonies in, and by which, its creed, tenet and dogma
are illustrated, by myth, symbols and allegories? If
Freemasonry were not religion, what would have to be done

Nothing would be necessary, or at least nothing but to add
more of the same (95:512, emphasis added).
Coil goes on to point out that not only does Freemasonry have a
creed, but that the Masonic Lodge actually functions as a church. For

That brings us to the real crux of the matter. The difference

Does the Masonic Lodge have its
own distinct doctrinal statement
like a church does?
Another reason Masons give for asserting that Freemasonry is not a
religion is because "we have no confession of faith in a doctrinal
statement such as a church does." But is this true?
Any Mason must honestly say he has a confession of faith: He must
believe in the teachings of the Landmarks concerning the universal
Fatherhood of God and brotherhood of man; he must believe in the
resurrection and immortality of the soul; he must believe in a Supreme
Being; and he must believe that as a good Mason he will reside in the
"Celestial Lodge Above" for all eternity.
As one of many possible illustrations of this, consider the
introductory material in the Heirloom Masonic Bible, an edition
published exclusively for Freemasonry. Speaking of the Third Degree
Ritual, the Heirloom Bible mentions three indispensable beliefs:
The doctrine of the resurrection of the body to a future and
eternal life constitutes an essential dogma of the religious
faith of Freemasonry. The requirement for adherence to this
doctrine holds equal rank with the demand for belief in Deity
and in the immortality of the soul (119:55).
Note the wording that follows, which emphasizes how essential
belief in the "resurrection of the body" is: "It is more authoritatively
inculcated in the symbolism of the Third Degree than is possible by
any dogmatic creed." Then, in regard to all three beliefs, the Heirloom
Bible declares,
Throughout the ritualisms, symbolisms, legends, and lectures
of the Order, these doctrines are affirmed (119:55).
Though Masons may not wish to call these beliefs a "creed," they are
nonetheless doctrinal requirements of Freemasonry.
Not only do Masons have a confession of faith in their own doctrinal
beliefs, but their beliefs are distinctive. It can be seen that Masonry

religions believe in the doctrine of the immortality of the soul like

Do all religious people believe in a single Supreme Being as the

At death, do all religious people believe as Masons do that they will

Christians nor Muslims believe in universal salvation, as Masons do.
In conclusion, it is clear that the Masonic Lodge does have its own

That's why Masonry must be considered to be teaching religion.

The "Legend of Hiram Abiff" is held to be one of the most important
portions of Masonic ritual; some refer to it as the very heart and
foundation of Masonry. Other Masons have even referred to Hiram

Hiram Abiff is not a biblical individual, as many Masons assume,
but an allegorical figure prominent in the Lodge's Third-Degree Ritual.
One Masonic manual explains,
The tragedy of Hiram Abif is the climax of the Master Mason
degree, the essence of Freemasonry, the foundation of its
philosophy. To understand its symbolism is to understand
Masonry; to miss its significance is to remain forever in outer
darkness (121:95).
During an allegorical play that is enacted as part of the Ritual,
Hiram is said to be the principal builder of King Solomon's famous
temple and the first Grand Master Mason. He alone knows the secrets
of Masonry, including the most important secret, the "Great Masonic
Word," the Name of God. When an attempt is made to force him to
divulge his secrets, he refuseshe actually prefers death. Hiram is then
murdered, and his body is clandestinely buried. However, King
Solomon himself institutes a search, and after Hiram's body is found,
through prayer, God raises him from the dead.

--- Bölüm/Sayfa 60 ---
The Masonic initiate is later informed that the allegory is a lesson
about imitating the character of Hiram-that is, being faithful to one's
word (at least when it comes to Masonic oaths, even to the point of

of life but the immortality of the soul, which presumably encourages
the initiate to make the most of his life in Masonry. An initiate is

Finally my brethren, let us imitate our Grand Master, Hiram
Abiff, in his virtuous conduct, his unfeigned piety to God,

messenger sent by our Supreme Grand Master, to translate us

Lodge above, where the Supreme Architect of the Universe

In other words, just as the Christian is to imitate Jesus, so the
